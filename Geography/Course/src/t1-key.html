Part A · Multiple Choice 2 points each

  1. a) Lithosphere, hydrosphere, biosphere, atmosphere, and interior
    p. 8 — the opening definition of the discipline.
  2. c) independent variable
    p. 12 — everything else must be held constant; those are the experimental controls. What changes as a result is the dependent variable.
  3. d) The Tropic of Capricorn
    p. 14 — the Equator is the only parallel that is a great circle. All other parallels, including both tropics and both polar circles, are small circles.
  4. a) the universe is expanding
    p. 24 — if every galaxy is moving away from every other galaxy, space itself must be expanding.
  5. c) an elliptical galaxy
    p. 30 — the color is the clue. Reddish and yellowish means old stars; blue means young stars still forming from gas and dust.
  6. d) during a supernova explosion
    p. 37 — fusing elements heavier than iron consumes more energy than it produces, so ordinary stars stop at iron. A supernova carries enough energy to make gold, silver, and uranium.
  7. a) Hadean, Archean, Proterozoic, Phanerozoic
    p. 45 — the first three together account for almost 90% of Earth’s history.
  8. b) Superposition
    p. 47 — in an undisturbed sequence, the layers on the bottom are the oldest.
  9. b) One
    p. 49 — a ratio of 1 means 50% parent and 50% daughter, which is exactly one half-life. A ratio of 3 (25% parent, 75% daughter) would be two half-lives.
  10. d) 1:24,000
    p. 53 — the smaller the denominator, the larger the scale, and the more detail is shown. A campus map is large-scale; a world map is small-scale.
  11. b) an equal-area projection such as Gall-Peters
    p. 61 — the reader is comparing quantities spread over areas, so relative area must be true even if shapes are distorted.
  12. c) 100 feet
    p. 55 — every fifth contour line is an index contour, and 5 × 20 ft = 100 ft. Index contours are the only ones labeled.

Part C · Short Answer 3 points each

  1. Relative dating establishes only the order of events — whether one rock is older or younger than another. Absolute dating assigns a numerical age in years. The discovery of radioactivity in the late 1800s made it possible.
    Rubric: 1 pt relative = order without numbers; 1 pt absolute = specific numerical age; 1 pt radioactivity (accept “radio-isotopic dating” or “isotopic dating techniques” developed in the early 20th century). (pp. 47–48)
  2. Because a great circle must divide the Earth into two equal halves, and the Equator is the only line of latitude that does so.
    Rubric: 2 pts for the definition of a great circle — any circle that divides the Earth into two equal halves, and the largest circle that can be drawn on a sphere. 1 pt for recognizing that every other parallel is smaller and cuts the Earth unevenly, making it a small circle. (p. 14)
  3. Remote sensing, global positioning systems (GPS), and geographic information systems (GIS).
    Rubric: 1 pt each, requiring both the name and a defensible use. Remote sensing — studying objects without physical contact, via satellite imagery, aerial photography, radar, or lidar. GPS — determining an accurate location on Earth’s surface using at least four satellites and triangulation. GIS — bringing spatial and non-spatial data together in layers for storage and analysis. (pp. 18–21)
  4. A Sun-like star stops fusion and collapses into a white dwarf. A much more massive star explodes as a supernova, leaving behind either a neutron star or a black hole.
    Rubric: 1.5 pts for the Sun-like path — red giant, then fusion stops, then gravitational collapse to a white dwarf about the size of Earth, which fades out. 1.5 pts for the massive path — red supergiant, fusion continues up to iron, then core collapse produces a supernova, leaving a neutron star (core under about four solar masses) or a black hole (core over about five solar masses). (pp. 36–38)

Part D · Extended Response 6 points each

  1. See rubric.
    Rubric: 2 pts — why distortion is unavoidable. A globe is three-dimensional and a map is two-dimensional, so flattening always requires shearing, tearing, or compression. The orange-peel analogy earns full credit here. Since no projection can preserve every property, some distortion is guaranteed.

    2 pts — the three properties. Shape, area, and distance. (Accept “shape and angle” for the first.)

    2 pts — how the choice is made, with two named projections. The cartographer decides which property matters most for the map’s purpose. Expect examples such as: Mercator (conformal — correct shapes and angles, badly distorted area; good for navigation, topography, weather); Gall-Peters (equal-area — true relative sizes, distorted shapes; good for density and thematic maps); Azimuthal Equidistant (correct distance and direction from a center point; good for flight paths or earthquake epicenters); Robinson (a compromise that minimizes all distortion rather than eliminating any; good for general-purpose world maps); Goode Homolosine (interrupted — accepts tearing to preserve area).

    Award full credit for any two correctly described projections tied to a purpose. (pp. 57–63)
