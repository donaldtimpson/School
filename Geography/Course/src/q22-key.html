ANSWER KEY  ·  Quiz 22 · Unit 18 (pages 260–273)20 points total

Part A · Multiple Choice 2 points each

  1. d) fine, dry, and loose, with no vegetation cover
    p. 261 — vegetation imposes a frictional drag on the wind and binds soil particles down, so an environment without it is best. Clay-rich soils resist wind erosion because clay is cohesive.
  2. a) About one-quarter
    p. 261 — in most deserts wind erosion predominates over deposition, leaving a surface of stones rather than sand.
  3. b) desert pavement
    p. 262 — also called desert lag. The interlocking stones protect the surface beneath, which is why military activity in North African deserts during World War II caused lasting degradation.
  4. b) downwind
    p. 267 — symmetrical barchans indicate wind from a constant direction. Parabolic dunes are the ones whose horns point upwind, anchored by vegetation or dampness.
  5. c) More than 50%
    pp. 263–264 — traction, or surface creep, accounts for the 20–25%. Sand generally begins moving at a wind velocity of about 4.5 m/sec.
  6. a) a playa
    p. 271 — there are more than a hundred in North American deserts, most relics of Ice Age lakes. Lake Bonneville left the Great Salt Lake, Utah Lake, and Sevier Lake behind.

Part B · True or False 1 point each

  1. TRUE
    p. 268 — dampness and plants slow the dune’s base while the dry crest pushes ahead, leaving the horns anchored behind and producing the hairpin shape.
  2. FALSE
    That is a butte. Geographers use the rule of thumb that a mesa’s top is wider than its height; a butte is what remains when a mesa’s caprock has caved away to a small remnant. (pp. 271–273)

Part C · Short Answer 3 points each

  1. Deflation is the lifting and removal of loose fine particles by the wind; abrasion is the sandblasting of rock surfaces by sand grains carried in the wind.
    Rubric: 1 pt deflation defined, 1 pt abrasion defined, 1 pt for a valid landform for each. Deflation produces desert pavement (desert lag) and blowout depressions; the Qattara Depression in Egypt is partly a deflation feature. Abrasion shapes and polishes exposed bedrock, producing ventifacts (smooth faceted rocks) and, at larger scale, yardangs — elongate ridges streamlined parallel to the prevailing wind, which archaeologists think partly form the Great Sphinx of Giza. Credit noting abrasion is limited to a meter or two above the surface, since sand grains are not lifted far. (pp. 262–263)
  2. Horizontally layered rocks are uplifted, then weathering and erosion strip the weaker layers away while a resistant caprock protects the rock beneath, leaving the resistant material standing above its surroundings.
    Rubric: 1 pt — horizontally layered rocks are uplifted by tectonic activity, and rock types differ in their ability to resist weathering and erosion. 1 pt — weaker rocks such as shale erode into valleys while resistant types (sandstone, conglomerate, quartzite, basalt, chert, limestone, lava flows, and sills) remain topographically higher; a resistant sill or lava flow often forms the flat caprock. 1 pt — the sides break into a cliff-and-bench staircase, and basal sapping erodes the soft shale beneath until the cliff collapses and retreats. When only a small remnant of caprock is left, the mesa has become a butte. (pp. 272–273)
