ANSWER KEY  ·  Quiz 8 · Unit 7a (pages 95–104)20 points total

Part A · Multiple Choice 2 points each

  1. a) the percentage of water vapor the air holds relative to the maximum it can hold at that temperature
    p. 96 — because warm air can hold more water vapor than cool air, simply raising or lowering the temperature changes the relative humidity even if no water is added or removed.
  2. b) dew point
    p. 96 — the name comes from dew, which is exactly this process happening at ground level. The cold glass of ice water is the book’s small-scale example.
  3. c) Cirrus
    pp. 97–98 — the prefix cirro- marks high clouds, alto- marks middle clouds, and stratus and nimbostratus are low clouds made nearly entirely of water droplets.
  4. d) moist and warm
    p. 101 — “maritime” means it formed over water and is therefore moist; “tropical” means it formed at low latitudes and is therefore warm. Continental polar (cP) is the opposite: dry and cold.
  5. a) a line of severe thunderstorms that forms along a cold front
    p. 102 — in the United States these form in spring and early summer in the Midwest, where maritime tropical air from the Gulf of Mexico meets continental polar air from Canada.
  6. b) advection fog
    p. 99 — advection fog forms from the surface contact of horizontal winds, so unlike radiation fog it can occur in windy conditions. Sea breezes then carry it onshore.

Part B · True or False 1 point each

  1. TRUE
    p. 104 — a front may become stationary when an air mass is stopped by a barrier such as a mountain range. It can bring days of rain, drizzle, and fog before breaking apart.
  2. FALSE
    With both cold and warm fronts, the air mass at the leading edge gives the front its name. A warm front marks the leading edge of moving warm air. (p. 101)

Part C · Short Answer 3 points each

  1. Denser cold air pushes underneath the warm air, forcing it upward and lowering the air pressure; this builds cumulus clouds and a line of showers or thunderstorms, with drier, clearing weather behind the front.
    Rubric: 1 pt mechanism — the denser cold air pushes up the warm air and air pressure decreases. 1 pt clouds and precipitation at the front — cumulus clouds, with winds blowing ice crystals off their tops into cirrostratus and cirrus; a line of rain showers, snow showers, or thunderstorms with blustery winds. 1 pt behind the front — the drier cold air mass arrives, precipitation stops, and skies turn clear or partly cloudy. Credit seasonal detail as a bonus: thunderstorms or tornadoes in spring and summer, heavy rain in autumn, frigid temperatures and heavy snow in winter. (pp. 101–102)
  2. An occluded front forms when a cold front catches up to a warm front, usually around a low-pressure system. From front to back the air masses are cold, warm, then cold again.
    Rubric: 1.5 pts for the mechanism — a cold front overtakes a warm front, typically around a low-pressure system, and the Coriolis effect curves the boundary toward the pole. 1.5 pts for the order: cold, warm, cold. Bonus, not required: if the third air mass is colder than both others it slides beneath them (cold occlusion); if warmer, it rides over them (warm occlusion). Weather is especially fierce right at the occlusion. (p. 103)
