ANSWER KEY  ·  Quiz 10 · Unit 8 (pages 115–124)20 points total

Part A · Multiple Choice 2 points each

  1. a) The number of protons in the nucleus
    p. 116 — all atoms of a particular element are identical, and the proton count is what fixes the identity.
  2. d) a cation with a positive charge
    p. 117 — losing a negatively charged electron leaves more protons than electrons, so the net charge is positive. Gaining an electron produces an anion.
  3. c) Silicates
    p. 116 — carbonates and native elements are also named as important, but chiefly for their usefulness to society rather than their abundance.
  4. a) streak does not vary
    p. 119 — two minerals of the same color may leave different colored streaks. Pyrite looks gold but streaks blackish, while real gold streaks golden yellow.
  5. b) Talc
    p. 121 — talc can be scratched by all other materials. The full scale runs talc, gypsum, calcite, fluorite, apatite, feldspar, quartz, topaz, corundum, diamond.
  6. b) cleavage
    pp. 121–122 — cleavage follows lines of weakness in the mineral’s structure. Calcite cleaves in three directions into rhombs, and galena into true cubes.

Part B · True or False 1 point each

  1. FALSE
    Color is the most eye-catching property but usually the least reliable. Pure quartz is colorless, while amethyst is the same mineral colored purple by traces of iron. (pp. 117–118)
  2. TRUE
    p. 123 — unlike cleavage, which follows structural planes of weakness. Quartz is known for fracturing into smooth curved surfaces.

Part C · Short Answer 3 points each

  1. It must occur naturally, be solid, have an ordered internal (crystallographic) structure, and have a fairly well-defined chemical composition.
    Rubric: 1 pt for each of any three. (1) Naturally occurring, formed by natural geological processes — this excludes anything made by human activity or by living beings, such as seashells or tungsten carbide. (2) A solid in its natural occurrence. (3) A well-defined crystallographic structure, or more generally an ordered atomic arrangement. (4) A fairly well-defined chemical composition. (p. 124)
  2. Cleavage is the tendency of a mineral to break along structural planes of weakness, producing smooth surfaces. Fracture is any break that does not follow a cleavage plane.
    Rubric: 1 pt cleavage defined — breakage along certain planes determined by the mineral’s internal structure, yielding smooth surfaces. 1 pt fracture defined — a break not along a cleavage plane, and therefore not fixed by structure. 1 pt for one valid example of each. Cleavage: mica (one plane, thin sheets), calcite (three planes, rhombs), galena or halite (three planes at right angles, cubes). Fracture: quartz (smooth curved surfaces), chrysotile (splintery), metals (jagged edges). (pp. 121–123)
